5.3 Wave Equations

Wave phenomena are ubiquitous in nature: water waves on the ocean surface, accoustic waves by which sound travels, electromagnetic waves through which our TV and radio sets receive their programs. Indeed, the forms of waves are multifaceted, but they are all described by the same type of mathematical equations, commonly called the wave equations.

An initial-boundary value problem for the wave equation

We consider an initial-boundary value probelm for the second-order one-dimensional wave equation,

{∂2u∂t2−c2⁢∂2u∂x2=0,0<x<L,t>0,u⁢(0,t)=u⁢(L,t)=0,u⁢(x,0)=f⁢(x),ut⁢(x,0)=g⁢(x).

The first equation is the second-order wave equation in the one-dimensional spatial domain, t standing for the time variable. The equation is posed on a finite interval [0,L] (Figure 5.4), and the equations in the second line describe the boundary conditions that the solution must satisfy. The equations in the third line describe the initial state of the system, and they are called the initial conditions. A problem where both the intial and boundary conditions are present are called an initial-boundary value problem.

Among other things, the system (1) can serve as a model for the vibration of a string. In this context, u=u⁢(x,t) represents the displacement of the string from rest, the boundary conditions specify that the string is fixed at its two end points at x=0 and x=L.

Facing this new problem, we have no choice but to apply the one technique that has worked quite well for us so far in this chapter, the separation of variable method.

Step 1. Separation of variables
We look for solutions that is in the form of product,

u⁢(x,t)=T⁢(t)⁢X⁢(x).

Replace u by T⁢X in the equation,

T′′⁢X−c2⁢T⁢X′′=0,T′′T=c2⁢X′′X.

The left-hand side depends on t only, while the right-hand side depends on x only, and the identity must hold for all t>0, 0<x<L. This is only possible if both sides equal a common constant, say λ.

T′′=λ⁢T,X′′=λc2⁢X.

Step 2. Solve the s-L problem for X
From the boundary conditions for u, one infers that for T⋅X to be a solution, the function X must satisfy

X⁢(0)=X⁢(L)=0.

Thus, the possible candidates for X⁢(x) are eigenfunctions of the S-L problem

{X′′−λc2⁢X=0,X⁢(0)=X⁢(L)=0.

Solving this problem, one finds

eigenvalues λn=−(n⁢π⁢cL)2, n≥1,
eigenfunctions Xn⁢(x)=sin⁡n⁢π⁢xL, n≥1.

Step 3. Solve the equation for T⁢(t)
With the same λ values from the above, the equation for T⁢(t) now reads

T′′+(n⁢π⁢cL)2⁢T=0.

The characteristic equation for this differential equation has two imaginary solutions,

±i⁢n⁢π⁢cL.

Thus T⁢(t), for each n≥1, has the general form

Tn⁢(t)=An⁢cos⁡n⁢π⁢c⁢tL+Bn⁢sin⁡n⁢π⁢c⁢tL.

Step 4. Assemble the final solution as a series

u⁢(x,t)=∑n≥1Tn⁢(t)⁢Xn⁢(t)=∑n≥1(An⁢cos⁡n⁢π⁢c⁢tL+Bn⁢sin⁡n⁢π⁢c⁢tL)⁢sin⁡n⁢π⁢xL.

We note that each Xn⁢(x) satisfy the homogeneous boundary conditions imposed at x=0 and x=L, and so does the constructed solution u⁢(x,t). The initial conditions at t=0 have not be touched at all, and we now use these conditions to determine the coefficients An and Bn in the series above. We set t=0 in u⁢(x,t) and ut⁢(x,t),

u⁢(x,0) =∑n≥1An⁢sin⁡n⁢π⁢xL=f⁢(x),
ut⁢(x,0) =∑n≥1Bn⁢n⁢π⁢cL⁢sin⁡n⁢π⁢xL=g⁢(x).

The above are simply sine series expansion for the given functions f⁢(x) and g⁢(x), and the coefficients An and Bn can be easily determined by our usual techniques,

An=2L⁢∫0Lf⁢(x)⁢sin⁡n⁢π⁢xL⁢d⁢x,
Bn=2n⁢π⁢c⁢∫0Lg⁢(x)⁢sin⁡n⁢π⁢xL⁢d⁢x.

To summarize, the solution to the initial-boundary-value problem (1) is given by (2), with An and Bn just given in the above.

 

What does the solution form (2) tell us about the wave phenomenon that it purports to describe? In fact, quite a bit. Below, we take the general form (2), without any information on the coefficients An and Bn, and we derive the propagation speed of the wave.

u⁢(x,t) =∑n≥1(An⁢cos⁡n⁢π⁢c⁢tL+Bn⁢sin⁡n⁢π⁢c⁢tL)⁢sin⁡n⁢π⁢xL
=∑n≥1(An⁢sin⁡n⁢π⁢xL⁢cos⁡n⁢π⁢c⁢tL+Bn⁢sin⁡n⁢π⁢xL⁢sin⁡n⁢π⁢c⁢tL)
=∑n≥1An2⁢(sin⁡n⁢πL⁢(x+c⁢t)+sin⁡n⁢πL⁢(x−c⁢t))
+Bn2⁢(cos⁡n⁢πL⁢(x−c⁢t)−cos⁡n⁢πL⁢(x+c⁢t))
=∑n≥1An2⁢sin⁡n⁢πL⁢(x+c⁢t)−Bn2⁢cos⁡n⁢πL⁢(x+c⁢t)
+An2⁢sin⁡n⁢πL⁢(x−c⁢t)+Bn2⁢cos⁡n⁢πL⁢(x−c⁢t)
=∑n≥1Cn⁢sin⁡(n⁢πL⁢(x+c⁢t)+ϕn)+Dn⁢sin⁡(n⁢πL⁢(x−c⁢t)+ψn).

Now it is clear that the wave exhibits two propagation modes:

Mode 1

moving to the left at speed c, represented by terms sin⁡(n⁢πL⁢(x+c⁢t)+ϕn), n≥1;

Mode 2

moving to the right at speed c, represented by terms sin⁡(n⁢πL⁢(x−c⁢t)+ψn), n≥1.

What is remarkable, from this analysis, is that wave forms of all frequencies traveling at the same uniform speed. In other words, the wave phenomenon described by the system (1) is non-dispersive.

Example 2. Waves under stiffness∗

(Recommended reading, but not required)
Consider the case where the string is embedded in a thin sheet of rubber, which exerts an external force that is proportional to the displacement of the string, but always in the opposite direction (stiffness). Thus the new model takes the form

∂2u∂t2=c2⁢∂2u∂x2−κ⁢u,0<x<L,t>0.

Assume that u⁢(x,t) satisfies the same initial and boundary conditions as in the previous example. Find the solution.

Solution:
Step 1. Separation of variables

u=T⁢X,
T′′⁢X=c2⁢T⁢X′′−κ⁢T⁢X,
T′′T=c2⁢X′′X−κ,
T′′T+κ=c2⁢X′′X=−λ.

Step 2. Solve the s-L problem for X

{X′′+λc2⁢X=0,X⁢(0)=X⁢(L)=0.
Xn=sin⁡n⁢π⁢xL,n≥1,
λn=(n⁢π⁢cL)2,n≥1.

Step 3. Solve the equation for T. For each n≥1,

T′′+((n⁢πL)2+κ)⁢T=0.
T⁢(t)=An⁢cos⁡(n2⁢π2⁢c2L2+κ⁢t)+Bn⁢sin⁡(n2⁢π2⁢c2L2+κ⁢t).

Step 4. Find the solution u⁢(x,t)

u⁢(x,t)=∑n≥1(An⁢cos⁡(n2⁢π2⁢c2L2+κ⁢t)+Bn⁢sin⁡(n2⁢π2⁢c2L2+κ⁢t))⁢sin⁡n⁢π⁢xL.

Apply the initial conditions, we find the same An & Bn as before,

An=32⁢hn2⁢π2⁢sin⁡(n⁢π2)⁢sin2⁡(n⁢π8),Bn=0.

Hence

u⁢(x,t)=∑n≥1An⁢sin⁡n⁢π⁢xL⁢cos⁡(n2⁢π2⁢c2L2+κ⁢t).

We note

u⁢(x,t) =∑n=1∞An2⁢(sin⁡(n⁢πL⁢x+n2⁢π2⁢c2L2+κ⁢t)+sin⁡(n⁢πL⁢x−n2⁢π2⁢c2L2+κ⁢t))
=∑n=1∞An2⁢sin⁡n⁢πL⁢(x+Ln⁢π⁢n2⁢π2⁢c2L2+κ⁢t)
+An2⁢sin⁡n⁢πL⁢(x−Ln⁢π⁢n2⁢π2⁢c2L2+κ⁢t).

Propagation speed

=±Ln⁢π⁢n2⁢π2⁢c2L2+κ.

The speed varies according to the frequency. Hence the wave is dispersive.

Example 3. Wave under damping∗

(Recommended reading, not required.)
Here we take into account the reaction of the environment to the motion of the string. For small-amplitude motions, the reaction opposes the motion, and is proportional to the element’s velocity. The wave equation is revised as follows,

∂2u∂t2=c2⁢∂2u∂x2−2⁢ν⁢∂u∂t.

Here ν>0 is a parameter representing the friction from the medium, which acts to damp the motion of the string, unlike the stiffness from a rubber sheet.

Solve the damped wave equation subjecting to the same initial and boundary conditions as in the previous examples, that is

u⁢(0,t)=u⁢(L,t)=0,
u⁢(x,0)=f⁢(x),ut⁢(x,0)=0.

Solution:
Step 1. Separation of variables

u⁢(x,t)=T⁢(t)⁢X⁢(x).

Step 2. Solve the S-L problem for X

Xn=sin⁡(n⁢π⁢xL),n≥1,
λn=n2⁢π2L2,n≥1.

Step 3. Solution for T For n≥1,

Tn⁢(t)=e−ν⁢t⁢[An⁢cos⁡(n2⁢π2⁢c2L2−ν2⁢t)+Bn⁢sin⁡(n2⁢π2⁢c2L2−ν2⁢t)]

assuming that

n⁢π⁢cL>ν.

Step 4. Assemble the final solution u⁢(x,t)

u⁢(x,t)=e−ν⁢t⁢∑sin⁡(n⁢π⁢xL)⁢[An⁢cos⁢()+Bn⁢sin⁢()].

An,Bn are exactly the same as before. In particular,

Bn=0,
u⁢(x,t)=e−ν⁢t⁢∑An⁢sin⁡n⁢π⁢xL⁢cos⁡(n2⁢π2⁢c2L2−ν2⁢t).
u⁢(x,t) =e−ν⁢t∑An2(sinn⁢πL(x−Ln⁢πn2⁢π2⁢c2L2−ν2t)
+sinn⁢πL(x+Ln⁢πn2⁢π2⁢c2L2−ν2t)).

Wave propagation speed

Ln⁢π⁢n2⁢π2⁢c2L2−ν2<c.

The result from this example shows that damping from the medium can

  • •

    slow down the propagation of the modes of all frequencies;

  • •

    lead to energy loss;

  • •

    lead to noise by causing waves to disperse.
