Properties of Fourier series

1. Term-by-term differentiation

If f⁢(t) is a continuous function on the real line with a period of 2⁢L and if its derivative f′⁢(t) is continuous except for a finite number of discontinuities, then the Fourier series of f′⁢(t) is given by a term-by-term differentiation of the Fourier series of f⁢(t).

Example 1. Consider a periodic function f⁢(t) with a period of 2⁢π and

f⁢(t)=|t|for ⁢|t|<2⁢π.

The Fourier series expansion for f⁢(t) is given by

f⁢(t)=π2−4π⁢∑m=1∞cos⁡((2⁢m−1)⁢t)(2⁢m−1)2.

The function f⁢(t) has derivative

f′⁢(t)={−1,−π≤t<0,1,0≤t<π.

f′⁢(t) has only one discontinuity within the interval [−π,π], and also meets other criteria set forth in Dirichlet theorem, and therefore it has a Fourier series expansion. Its Fourier series can be obtained through direct calculations:

a0=1π⁢∫−ππf′⁢(t)⁢𝑑t=1π⁢(∫−π0(−1)⁢𝑑t+∫0π1⁢𝑑t)=0,
an=1π⁢∫−ππf′⁢(t)⁢cos⁡(n⁢π⁢tπ)⁢𝑑t=0,
bn =1π⁢∫−ππf′⁢(t)⁢sin⁡(n⁢π⁢tπ)⁢𝑑t=1π⁢(∫−π0(−1)⁢sin⁡n⁢t⁢d⁢t+∫0πsin⁡n⁢t⁢d⁢t)
=1π⁢(cos⁡n⁢tn|−π0−cos⁡n⁢tn|0π)=2⁢(1−(−1)n)n⁢π.

Then

f′⁢(t)=∑n=1∞2⁢(1−(−1)n)n⁢π⁢sin⁡(n⁢π⁢tπ)=∑m=1∞4(2⁢m−1)⁢π⁢sin⁡((2⁢m−1)⁢t)=4π⁢∑m=1∞sin⁡((2⁢m−1)⁢t)2⁢m−1.

A term-by-term differentiation of the Fourier series of f⁢(t) will yield the same series as the above.

2. Term-by-term integration

Let f⁢(t) has Fourier series

f⁢(t)=a02+∑n=1∞[an⁢cos⁡(n⁢π⁢tL)+bn⁢sin⁡(n⁢π⁢tL)].

Let F⁢(t) be a form of the antiderivative of f⁢(t). Then what does the Fourier series of F⁢(t) look like?

Example 2. Consider

f⁢(t)=t,−π<t<π.

This function f⁢(t) has Fourier series

f⁢(t)=−2⁢∑n=1∞(−1)nn⁢sin⁡n⁢t.

How to compute the Fourier series of t2?

We note that

t2=2×12⁢t2=2×∫t⁢𝑑t.

Does this help?

Back to the general question. Consider a form of integral of f⁢(t),

F⁢(t)=∫0t(f⁢(τ)−a02)⁢𝑑τ,

where a0 is the constant term in the Fourier series expansion of f⁢(t). It is easy to see that

F′⁢(t)=f⁢(t)−a02.

The newly constructed function F⁢(t) is also periodic, with a period =2⁢L, because

F⁢(t+2⁢L) =∫0t+2⁢L(f⁢(τ)−a02)⁢𝑑τ
=∫0t(f⁢(τ)−a02)⁢𝑑τ+∫tt+2⁢L(f⁢(τ)−a02)⁢𝑑τ
=F⁢(t)+[∫tt+2⁢Lf⁢(τ)⁢𝑑τ−L⁢a0]
=F⁢(t).

Based on the last observation, the function F⁢(t) has a Fourier series expansion, which we write as

F⁢(t)=A02+∑n=1∞[An⁢cos⁡(n⁢π⁢tL)+Bn⁢sin⁡(n⁢π⁢tL)].

The constant coefficient A0 can be found via direct calculations,

A0=1L⁢∫−LLF⁢(t)⁢𝑑t,

as F⁢(t) is known to us. We determine the other coefficients by applying the formulas derived in the previous sections, with

An =1L⁢∫−LLF⁢(t)⁢cos⁡(n⁢π⁢tL)⁢𝑑t
=1L⁢{F⁢(t)⁢sin⁡(n⁢π⁢tL)n⁢πL|−LL−∫−LLsin⁡(n⁢π⁢tL)n⁢πL⁢F′⁢(t)⁢𝑑t}
=−1L⋅Ln⁢π⁢∫−LLsin⁡(n⁢π⁢tL)⁢(f⁢(t)−a02)⁢𝑑t
=−Ln⁢π⁢bn.

In the above, integration by parts has been carried, and bn’s are coefficients of the Fourier series expansion of the original function f⁢(t). The other coefficients Bn are calcualted in a similar way,

Bn =1L⁢∫−LLF⁢(t)⁢sin⁡(n⁢π⁢tL)⁢𝑑t
=−1L⁢[F⁢(t)⁢cos⁡(n⁢π⁢tL)−n⁢πL]−LL+1L⁢∫−LLcos⁡(n⁢π⁢tL)n⁢πL⁢F′⁢(t)⁢𝑑t
=1L⋅Ln⁢π⁢∫−LL(f⁢(t)−a02)⁢cos⁡(n⁢π⁢tL)⁢𝑑t
=Ln⁢π⁢an.

We now turn back to our Example 2 in the above.

Solution (of Example 2): The function f⁢(t)=t over [−π,π] has Fourier series expansion

f⁢(t)=−2⁢∑n=1∞(−1)nn⁢sin⁡n⁢t.

In this series expansion,

L=π,

and

a0=0,an=0,bn=−2⁢(−1)nn=2⁢(−1)n+1n.

We construct the integral according to the formula given in the above,

F⁢(t)=∫0t(f⁢(τ)−a02)⁢𝑑τ=∫0tτ⁢𝑑τ=t22.

The coefficients of the Fourier series expansion of this function can be computed using the formulas that have just been derived in the above,

A0=1π⁢∫−ππt22⁢𝑑t=1π⁢t36|−ππ=2⁢π3π×6=π23.
An=−Ln⁢π⁢bn=−πn⁢π⋅2⁢(−1)n+1n=2⁢(−1)nn2.
Bn=0.

Thus, the function F⁢(t)=t22 has Fourier series expansion

t22=π26+∑n=1∞2⁢(−1)nn2⁢cos⁡n⁢t.

Multiplying both sides by a factor of 2, we obtain the Fourier series expansion that this example asks for t2,

t2=π23+4⁢∑n=1∞(−1)nn2⁢cos⁡n⁢t.

 

We note that this property of Fourier series is called term-by-term integration, because

An=−Ln⁢π⁢bn

results from an integration of the term

bn⁢sin⁡(n⁢π⁢tL),

and

Bn=Ln⁢π⁢an

results from an integration of the term

an⁢cos⁡(n⁢π⁢tL).

In fact, if we integrate

f⁢(t)−a02=∑n=1∞[an⁢cos⁡(n⁢π⁢tL)+bn⁢sin⁡(n⁢π⁢tL)]

over [0,t], we have

∫0t(f⁢(τ)−a02)⁢𝑑τ =∑n=1∞an⁢(Ln⁢π)⏟Bn⁢sin⁡(n⁢π⁢tL)
−∑n=1∞bn⁢(Ln⁢π)⏟−An⁢cos⁡(n⁢π⁢tL)+∑n=1∞bn⁢(Ln⁢π)⏟A0/2,

and the same set of formulas result.

3. Parseval’s equality

Many quantities in physics or engineering take the quadratic power forms, such as kinetic energy, which is a quadratic of velocity. Here, we study how quadratic quantities can be calculated from their Fourier series expansions. The quadratic power of a periodic function f⁢(t) on [τ,τ+2⁢L] is defined as

1L⁢∫ττ+2⁢L|f⁢(t)|2⁢𝑑t. (1)

This mathematical expression mirrors the definition for kinetic energy, 12⁢m⁢v2, in physics.

Assume that the function f⁢(t) has Fourier expansion

f⁢(t)=a02+∑n=1∞(an⁢cos⁡(n⁢π⁢tL)+bn⁢sin⁡(n⁢π⁢tL)). (2)

Then the power of f⁢(t) on [τ,τ+2⁢L] can be calculated as

1L⁢∫ττ+2⁢L|f⁢(t)|2⁢𝑑t
= 1L⁢∫ττ+2⁢Lf⁢(t)⋅f⁢(t)⁢𝑑t
= 1L⁢∫ττ+2⁢Lf⁢(t)⁢(a02+∑n=1∞(an⁢cos⁡(n⁢π⁢tL)+bn⁢sin⁡(n⁢π⁢tL)))⁢𝑑t
= a02⁢1L⁢∫ττ+2⁢Lf⁢(t)⁢𝑑t+∑n=1∞(an⁢1L⁢∫ττ+2⁢Lf⁢(t)⁢cos⁡(n⁢π⁢tL)⁢𝑑t+bn⁢1L⁢∫ττ+2⁢Lf⁢(t)⁢sin⁡(n⁢π⁢tL)⁢𝑑t).

We note that the integrals on the right-hand side turn out to be the formulas for the Fourier series coefficients themselves! Hence, we end up with the relation that

1L⁢∫ττ+2⁢L|f⁢(t)|2⁢𝑑t=a022+∑n=1∞(an2+bn2).

This is Parseval’s equality. It allows us to calculate the power of a function by summing up the squares of its Fourier coefficients.

Example 3. The Fourier series for

f⁢(t)=t,t∈[−π,π]

is

f⁢(t)=∑n=1∞2⁢(−1)n+1n⁢sin⁡(n⁢t).

By Parseval’s equality,

1π⁢∫−ππt2⁢𝑑t=∑n=1∞4n2.

The left-hand side of this equation can be evaluated easily,

1π⁢∫−ππt2⁢𝑑t=1π⁢t33|−ππ=23⁢π2.

Thus we have reached an identity that is quite unusual,

23⁢π2=∑n=1∞4n2⟹∑n=1∞1n2=π26.

It tells us that the infinite series involving the inverse of the square of all natural numbers, unlike the infamous harmonic series 1+12+13+⋯, actually converges, to a very special number: π26!

4. Gibbs phenomena∗

We begin with an example. The function

f⁢(t)={−1,−π<t≤0,1,0<t≤π.

has Fourier series

f⁢(t)=4π⁢∑m=1∞sin⁡[(2⁢m−1)⁢t]2⁢m−1.

As we have done before, we plot a few finite sums of this series (Figure 13), and observe their convergence to the underlying function. As the number of terms included increases, the finite sum does converge to the underlying function in most places. The only troublesome spots are places where the underlying functions have jumps (discontinuities), and the graphs of the finite sums exhibit some humps. What’s worrisome is that the magnitudes of those humps appear to persist, even as the number of terms in the finite sum increases; the only change, as the number goes up, is that the humps appear to move closer to the discontinuous points. Given that the magnitudes of those humps appear to persist, is the convergence conclusion of the Dirichlet theorem still valid?

What’s just observed on the graphs of Fourier series around the discontinuity points are called Gibbs phenomenon. In this section, we want to perform an analysis on the Fourier series in order to gain some understanding of what is happening here.

Let f⁢(t) be a function on [0,2⁢π] with Fourier series

f⁢(t)=a02+∑n=1∞(an⁢cos⁡(n⁢t)+bn⁢sin⁡(n⁢t)). (1)

The coefficients are given by

a0=1π⁢∫02⁢πf⁢(t)⁢𝑑t,
an=1π⁢∫02⁢πf⁢(t)⁢cos⁡(n⁢t)⁢𝑑t,
bn=1π⁢∫02⁢πf⁢(t)⁢sin⁡(n⁢t)⁢𝑑t.

The Fourier series (1) is infinite, but in practice we can only deal with finite numbers of terms. Let SN be a partial sum of the first N terms from the Fourier series,

SN⁢(t)=a02+∑n=1N(an⁢cos⁡(n⁢t)+bn⁢sin⁡(n⁢t)).

Then, substituting the formulas for a0, an, and bn into the right-hand side, we have

SN⁢(t) =12⁢π⁢∫02⁢πf⁢(x)⁢𝑑x+∑n=1N[1π⁢∫02⁢πf⁢(x)⁢cos⁡(n⁢x)⁢𝑑x⁢cos⁡(n⁢t)+1π⁢∫02⁢πf⁢(x)⁢sin⁡(n⁢x)⁢𝑑x⁢sin⁡(n⁢t)]
=1π⁢∫02⁢πf⁢(x)⁢(12+∑n=1N(cos⁡(n⁢x)⁢cos⁡(n⁢t)+sin⁡(n⁢x)⁢sin⁡(n⁢t)))⁢𝑑x
=1π⁢∫02⁢πf⁢(x)⁢(12+∑n=1Ncos⁡n⁢(x−t))⁢𝑑x.

To reach the last line, of course the sum-difference formula for cos⁡(n⁢(x−t)) has been used. We also note that

12+∑n=1Ncos⁡n⁢(x−t)=sin⁡((N+12)⁢(x−t))2⁢sin⁡(12⁢(x−t)).

Hence, the N-term partial sum SN⁢(t) can be expressed by a single integral,

SN⁢(t)=12⁢π⁢∫02⁢πf⁢(x)⁢sin⁡((N+12)⁢(x−t))sin⁡(12⁢(x−t))⁢𝑑x.

The factor other than f⁢(t) in the integrand is the scanning function, and the partial sums behave the way they do because of this function. We note that, for |x−t|≪1,

sin⁡((N+12)⁢(x−t))sin⁡(12⁢(x−t))∼(N+12)⁢(x−t)12⁢(x−t)=2⁢N+1.

This is also the maximum value for the scanning function over −π≤x−t≤π.

To have a direct visual of the this function, we plot it, for N=1, 2, 4, 8, and x=0, in Figure 14. The plottings confirm that the function does peak at 2⁢N+1, just as the analysis predicted. What stands out, as N increases, is that there are ripples below zero, and the ripples move closer towards the center, but do not diminish. In fact, the magnitudes of these ripples only increase as N increases. This observation is confirmed by examining the areas of various regions under the graph (Figure 15). The area under the graph over the entire interval is 2⁢π, as expected; the area under the center peak region is about 7.4, always greater than 2⁢π, which means that area enclosed by the curve below the t-axis remains strictly positive, regardless of the value of N.
