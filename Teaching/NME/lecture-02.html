Representation of Numbers in Computers

The binary format

As is well known, modern electrical computers use a binary format to store numbers, whether they are integers or decimals. Thus, any number is just a sequence of zeros and ones,

Due to the limitations on the amount of resources available, each and every number, be it an integer or a real number, can only be represented by a finite number of binary digits, and this fact has profound implications:

  • •

    Each number can be represented with only finite precision;

  • •

    The number of numbers representable on a computer is actually finite.

On the second point, it can be noted that, in a system where 32-bit is used for one integer, the largest integer representable within this system is about

232≈10003≈109.

The first point is well illustrated by the following two examples.

Example 1 (The peril of adding numbers of disparaging magnitudes)

Try 1+10−16 and 10−14+10−16 on Matlab.

Solution.

Matlab store real numbers up to the 16th digit (significant decimal). Thus, in Matlab,

1 =1.000 000 000 000 000
10−16 =0.000 000 000 000 000 1⁢0⁢⋯⁢0⏟15⁢ more.

Thus, Matlab has no difficulty in representing these numbers, but it really struggles in adding them together, because it can’t properly store the result

1.000 000 000 000 000 1

within its 16-digit limit. To fit in the limit, it has no choice but to drop the last digit from the result, resulting in 1 as the final answer.

On the other hand,

10−14 =0.000 000 000 000 010⁢0⁢⋯⁢0⏟14⁢ more,
10−16 =0.000 000 000 000 000 1⁢0⁢⋯⁢0⏟15⁢ more.

Again, Matlab has no difficulty in representing these numbers individually. It can also safely add them together, for the result

10−14+10−16=0.000 000 000 000 010 10⁢⋯⁢0

can be adequately stored within its 16-digit limit. ∎

Conversion between binary and decimal numbers

Binary → decimal: easy, just done it.

The following is a procedure for converting decimals → binaries, d denoting a positive decimal number:

  1. 1.

    Find the biggest power p of 2 such that 2p≤d (p may be negative). Save the index number p.

  2. 2.

    Set d=d−2p.

  3. 3.

    If d=0 or smaller than a preset tolerance, stop. Otherwise repeat steps 1 & 2.

For the binary number, put 1’s where you save the index numbers, and put 0’s elsewhere.

Example 5.

Convert the base 10 number d=11.5625 to base 2.

Solution.

Following the procedure from the above,

  1. 1.

    Find the biggest power p such that 2p≤11.5625: p=3, for (24>11.5625).

  2. 2.

    Update d=11.5625−23=3.5625.

  3. 3.

    Find the biggest power p s.t. 2p≤3.5625: p=1.

  4. 4.

    Update d=3.5625−21=1.5625.

  5. 5.

    Find the biggest power p s.t.  2p≤1.5625: p=0.

  6. 6.

    Update d=1.5625−20=.5625.

  7. 7.

    Find p s.t. 2p≤0.5625: p=−1.

  8. 8.

    d=.5625−2−1=.0625.

  9. 9.

    Find p: p=−4, 2−4=.0625.

  10. 10.

    d=.0625−.0625=0. STOP.

To finish up, we list all the indices, with those saved in the above marked by an underline, and put 1’s at their places,

Index3¯21¯0¯−1¯−2−3−4¯Digits1011.1001

Hence the number in the binary format is

(1011.1001)2.

∎

Standard binary form

Every binary number can be put in the standard binary form,

x=±1.b1⁢b2⁢b3⁢⋯×2p

Here, b1,b2,b3,… are binary digits 0 or 1; p is an integer (positive or negative).

The IEEE double-precision standard

The 1985 IEEE standard specifies the formats for double-precision, single-precision and extended-precision representation of real number by computers. The double-precision uses 64 bits; single-precision uses 32 bits.

In the double-precision representation

Here,

s: sign, ⁢0⁢ for positive, ⁢1⁢ for negative;
c: exponent, ⁢0≤c≤211−1=2047,
c=0, 2047⁢ reserved for special values like 0, inf, NaN,
p=c−1023⁢ when⁢1≤c≤2046,the shifted exponent for normalized reals;
f: the mantissa,≡b12+b222+⋯+b52252.

The normalized real number represented by these 64 binary digits is given by the formula

r=(−1)s⁢(1+f)⁢2c−1023.

Consequences of this representation system

  • ∗

    There are biggest and smallest representable positive numbers:

    Biggest positive number =(2−2−52)×22046−1023
    ≈2×21023≈10308,
    Smallest positive number =1×21−1023=2−1022≈10−308.
  • ∗

    There are gaps between numbers. Given a number

    1.b1⁢b2⁢⋯⁢b52×2exponent,

    the smallest increment you can add to it is on the 52nd digit of the mantissa, which is

    2−52×2exponent.
  • ∗

    Gaps normalized by the magnitudes are called the relative spacing, also called the machine precision, and it is denoted as

    εmach=2−52≈2.22×10−16.

    The relative spacing is uniform across the real axis, and the fact that it is on the order of 10−16 indicates that 52 binary digits of the mantissa correspond to roughly 16 decimal digits. This is why Matlab, or pretty much any modern computer softwares, can only print out a real number up to the 16th digit!

  • ∗

    Any numbers between two adjacent computer numbers are not representable under this system, and must be rounded.
